# Visual Design Bible — v0.1

> Project: Telos Game · Saved: 2026-09-05 · Status: working art-direction reference
>
> Source: “Generate Realistic Art Deco Knight” conversation (6a9c77c3-7d80-83e8-aae1-c7dea03a02a0). Sections 1–34 preserve the original v0.1 design bible; the dated appendix records the subsequent ComfyUI visual tests.

## 1. Core identity

The world is a **physically grounded retro-industrial future imagined through late-20th-century Japanese animation**.

Everything should feel:

- engineered
- heavy
- tactile
- used
- mechanically understandable
- visually graphic
- somewhat anachronistic

The world should not feel like sleek contemporary sci-fi. It should feel like a future designed by people who trusted steel, ceramics, hydraulics, switches, optics, paper manuals, and giant machinery.

A useful north-star sentence:

> A massive, lived-in industrial future rendered with the visual clarity, material weight, and dramatic composition of hand-painted 1980s–1990s Japanese OVA animation.

---

# 2. The three-tier rule system

The style is divided into:

### Invariants
Must remain consistent almost everywhere.

### Dialects
Can vary by faction, region, class, or technology.

### Free variables
Can change dramatically between levels.

This distinction is what prevents the art direction from becoming restrictive.

---

# 3. Invariants

These are the strongest rules.

## 3.1 Physicality

Objects must feel constructed.

Favor visible evidence of:

- joints
- fasteners
- seams
- hinges
- cooling
- power transfer
- access panels
- cables
- bearings
- hydraulics
- maintenance

Even fantastical technology should suggest a physical implementation.

A useful test:

> Could a mechanic point to parts of this object and explain what they do?

Not every detail needs a real engineering purpose, but the object should imply one.

Avoid unexplained visual noise.

---

## 3.2 Mass

Important objects should convey weight.

Characters, machinery, buildings, doors, weapons, vehicles, and infrastructure should rarely feel delicate unless that delicacy is intentional.

Mass can come from:

- thick structural members
- large plates
- broad silhouettes
- visible support structures
- slow-moving mechanisms
- deep shadows
- proportionally small humans

Doors should look heavy enough that you wonder how they move.

---

## 3.3 Large forms before detail

The visual hierarchy should usually follow:

**70% primary forms  
20% secondary forms  
10% surface detail**

The silhouette should work before panel lines or decoration are added.

Bad:

> generic shape covered in thousands of small sci-fi panels

Good:

> powerful silhouette with a few meaningful details

---

## 3.4 Material honesty

Materials should behave like materials.

Metal should:

- scratch
- dent
- discolor
- oxidize
- polish where touched

Paint should:

- chip
- fade
- pool near seams
- reveal primer or metal

Concrete should:

- crack
- stain
- absorb water

Fabric should:

- sag
- crease
- fray
- collect dirt

Surfaces should communicate history.

---

# 4. Shape language

The global shape language is:

> **large geometric masses intersected by functional mechanical structures**

Favor:

- wedges
- cylinders
- armored curves
- truncated cones
- strong diagonals
- rectangular structural frames
- broad curved plates

Avoid excessive use of:

- ultra-thin surfaces
- arbitrary spikes
- tiny repetitive greebles
- perfectly smooth aerodynamic shells

Curves are allowed, but they should feel fabricated rather than organic unless the object actually is biological.

---

# 5. Industrial complexity

Mechanical detail should cluster around areas where something happens.

For example:

A shoulder joint:

- bearings
- hydraulic lines
- armor overlap
- actuators

A flat chest plate:

- comparatively quiet surface

This creates **detail gradients**.

Dense:

> joints, engines, weapons, interfaces

Sparse:

> structural armor, walls, hull surfaces

This is crucial for readability.

---

# 6. Rendering language

The rendering identity should evoke painted animation without requiring literal imitation of vintage production methods.

## Shadows

Favor:

- large hard shadow shapes
- two- or three-stage value grouping
- clear silhouettes
- restrained ambient fill

Avoid excessively smooth gradients everywhere.

---

## Highlights

Highlights should often be deliberately graphic.

Metal may have:

- sharp painted streaks
- broad reflective bands
- small intense hot spots

Not everything should behave like physically perfect ray-traced material.

The goal is:

> plausible material + intentional illustration.

---

## Linework

Use line weight selectively.

Thicker:

- silhouette
- major overlaps
- foreground forms

Thinner:

- mechanical detail
- surface markings

Not every edge needs an outline.

---

# 7. Texture and image character

The image should not appear digitally sterile.

Use subtle:

- grain
- paint variation
- texture breakup
- color noise
- lens bloom
- atmospheric softness

Avoid making every asset equally dirty.

Wear should correspond to use.

---

# 8. Color philosophy

Do not define one universal palette.

Define a **palette structure**.

A typical scene:

- 60–75% structural neutrals
- 15–30% regional/environmental color
- 5–10% high-saturation accent

Structural colors:

- charcoal
- steel
- faded white
- brown-black
- gunmetal
- concrete
- muted olive

Accent colors can be aggressive:

- vermilion
- cadmium yellow
- cyan
- electric blue
- signal orange
- deep violet

The contrast between dull infrastructure and saturated accents is important.

---

# 9. Color has function

Whenever possible, colors should mean something.

Possible world conventions:

**Red**
- military priority
- danger
- heat
- weapons

**Yellow**
- industrial hazard
- machinery
- maintenance

**Blue**
- instrumentation
- cooling
- electronics

**White**
- medical
- scientific
- ceremonial

**Green**
- logistics
- environmental control
- agricultural systems

These conventions can be broken culturally, but they give the world internal logic.

---

# 10. Canonical material families

Create a limited family of reusable materials.

Initial set:

### Painted steel
Most common manufactured surface.

### Blackened steel
Weapons, chassis, military infrastructure.

### Cast aluminum
Mechanical housings and older machinery.

### Oxidized brass
Prestige machinery and old institutions.

### Glazed ceramic
Advanced heat-resistant technology.

### Reinforced concrete
Architecture and fortifications.

### Heavy rubber
Seals, joints, boots, industrial flooring.

### Canvas
Covers, uniforms, equipment storage.

### Smoked glass
Sensors, optics, cockpits.

### Phosphor illumination
Instrumentation and displays.

Reusing these creates subconscious cohesion.

---

# 11. Technology language

Technology should feel **electromechanical before digital**.

Prefer:

- physical switches
- rotary controls
- indicator lamps
- printed labels
- gauges
- oscilloscopes
- CRTs
- mechanical counters
- magnetic tape
- ruggedized keyboards
- removable modules
- exposed cooling
- analog backup systems

Advanced systems may exist underneath all of this.

The sophistication should come from capability, not sleek appearance.

A machine can be astonishingly intelligent while looking like something built in 1987.

---

# 12. UI and interface design

World UI should feel physical.

Favor:

- phosphor monitors
- segmented displays
- monochrome terminals
- vector graphics
- waveform displays
- grid overlays
- telemetry
- printed diagrams
- technical symbology

Typography:

- condensed grotesk
- industrial stencil
- monospaced technical fonts
- occasional hand-painted markings

Avoid:

- translucent floating rectangles
- excessive glassmorphism
- generic holographic interfaces

---

# 13. Architecture

Architecture should express infrastructure.

Buildings should show:

- ducts
- conduits
- vents
- elevators
- catwalks
- structural ribs
- service corridors
- cranes
- cooling towers

Architecture can differ dramatically across regions.

The common thread is:

> you can see how civilization functions.

---

# 14. Environmental diversity

The style must support extremes.

Valid environments include:

- dense megacity
- pastoral agricultural settlement
- orbital dockyard
- flooded industrial zone
- jungle refinery
- polar base
- desert fortress
- subterranean reactor
- alpine monastery
- underwater complex
- biological research facility
- abandoned colony ship
- aristocratic capital
- ruined ancient machine city

They do not need to share architecture.

They need to share **material logic, rendering logic, and world logic**.

---

# 15. Nature

Nature should provide contrast rather than simply inherit the industrial style.

Vegetation can be:

- lush
- chaotic
- delicate
- colorful

Rock formations can be:

- enormous
- pristine
- weathered

The stronger the natural environment, the more interesting industrial objects become inside it.

A giant yellow excavator in a green jungle is more memorable than a yellow excavator in another factory.

---

# 16. Vehicles

Vehicles should communicate their operating principle.

Ask:

- where does propulsion happen?
- where is fuel stored?
- how does the pilot enter?
- how is heat removed?
- where does suspension move?
- where are sensors located?

Vehicles should have distinctive silhouettes first.

Good archetypes:

- squat armored crawler
- tall walking artillery platform
- massive cargo lifter
- skeletal reconnaissance craft
- heavily enclosed personnel carrier

Avoid making everything a variation of the same tank.

---

# 17. Mecha and powered armor

These should feel like machines you wear or operate, not humanoid sculptures.

Key qualities:

- joint complexity
- armor overlapping mechanisms
- disproportionate mechanical strength
- visible heat management
- service access
- pilot protection

Armor can exaggerate anatomy, but should not simply reproduce human muscles in metal.

---

# 18. Characters

Humans should generally look smaller and softer than their machines.

Clothing can combine:

- workwear
- military uniforms
- technical garments
- ceremonial pieces
- regional clothing

Common visual elements:

- heavy fabric
- straps
- patched clothing
- practical footwear
- insignia
- personal modifications

Avoid making every character heavily armored.

That would diminish the effect of armor.

---

# 19. Factions as dialects

Every faction inherits the global grammar.

Then it modifies perhaps 3–5 properties.

Example:

## Central military

Geometry:
- broad
- symmetrical
- imposing

Materials:
- dark steel

Palette:
- black + crimson

Surface:
- standardized

Markings:
- large white stencils

Maintenance:
- disciplined but visibly used

---

## Industrial guild

Geometry:
- cylindrical
- modular

Materials:
- yellow-painted steel
- cast aluminum

Palette:
- yellow + dark gray

Surface:
- heavily serviced

Markings:
- numbered components

---

## Elite political class

Geometry:
- vertical
- symmetrical
- restrained

Materials:
- black ceramic
- brass

Palette:
- black + gold + ivory

Surface:
- highly maintained

Markings:
- heraldic rather than utilitarian

---

## Frontier settlements

Geometry:
- asymmetrical
- improvised

Materials:
- reused steel
- wood
- fabric

Palette:
- faded regional colors

Surface:
- heavily repaired

Markings:
- hand-painted

Same universe. Different dialect.

---

# 20. Signage and markings

This is potentially a major identity element.

Use:

- serial codes
- vehicle identifiers
- maintenance markings
- hazard stripes
- faction symbols
- inspection stamps
- hand-painted annotations
- direction arrows

Some objects may accumulate multiple generations of markings.

This implies institutional history.

---

# 21. Scale language

Regularly include objects whose scale exceeds immediate necessity.

Examples:

- absurdly large blast doors
- kilometer-long industrial corridors
- gigantic antennas
- enormous elevators
- cathedral-sized generators
- massive cooling stacks

The civilization should sometimes feel like it builds infrastructure for centuries.

But not every scene should be gigantic.

Small domestic spaces make the massive environments feel larger.

---

# 22. Lighting

Lighting should feel motivated.

Preferred sources:

- sunlight
- industrial lamps
- sodium vapor
- fluorescent fixtures
- furnace glow
- CRT illumination
- emergency lighting
- vehicle headlights

Use environmental color temperature deliberately.

Examples:

Industrial interior:

> green-gray ambient + orange work lamps

Frozen exterior:

> blue diffuse light + warm interior windows

Military hangar:

> dark neutral environment + intense red warning lights

---

# 23. Atmospheric effects

Atmosphere is part of the design language.

Use:

- steam
- dust
- fog
- rain
- snow
- smoke
- welding sparks
- exhaust
- condensation

But they should usually have environmental justification.

---

# 24. Composition

Reference late-20th-century animation cinematography rather than modern game marketing art.

Favor:

- strong foreground objects
- dramatic low angles
- long lenses
- deep perspective
- partial occlusion
- asymmetrical framing
- extreme scale relationships

Not every scene needs to look like a hero shot.

Quiet compositions are important too.

---

# 25. Beauty through function

A central philosophical rule:

> ornament should often emerge from engineering.

Examples:

- cooling fins become architectural rhythm
- structural ribs become facade decoration
- ventilation grilles form geometric patterns
- repeating bolts create visual order

This avoids generic decorative sci-fi.

---

# 26. Historical layering

The world should rarely look like everything was built simultaneously.

Show:

- old infrastructure beneath newer machinery
- abandoned standards
- obsolete connectors
- faded signage
- retrofitted systems
- repaired walls
- machinery adapted to new purposes

A civilization becomes believable when you can see its previous versions.

---

# 27. Anti-rules

Unless deliberately breaking the aesthetic, avoid:

- smooth Apple-like industrial design
- sterile white sci-fi corridors
- random neon everywhere
- excessive transparent holograms
- floating screens with no hardware
- meaningless greebles
- uniformly pristine materials
- superhero-style nanotech
- modern tacticool equipment everywhere
- overuse of RGB lighting
- glossy plastic surfaces
- extremely thin mechanical structures

---

# 28. Controlled rule breaking

Any rule can be broken if the contrast has narrative meaning.

For example:

A truly alien technology could be:

- perfectly smooth
- impossibly thin
- clean
- silent

Because everything else is mechanical and heavy, this suddenly becomes disturbing.

Breaking the rules works precisely because the rules exist.

---

# 29. Environment construction recipe

For each new level, define six things:

**Environment**
What physical place is this?

**Primary geometry**
What shapes dominate?

**Material family**
What was this place built from?

**Palette**
What color world does it occupy?

**Infrastructure**
What systems keep this place alive?

**Local contradiction**
What makes this environment visually unique?

Example:

### Jungle hydroelectric city

Environment:
tropical river canyon

Geometry:
vertical pylons + enormous circular turbines

Materials:
painted steel + wet concrete

Palette:
emerald + charcoal + warning yellow

Infrastructure:
pipes, turbines, sluice gates, suspension cranes

Contradiction:
delicate tropical vegetation growing through monumental machinery

Now it immediately has character.

---

# 30. AI prompt architecture

Instead of one giant style prompt, build prompts from modules.

### Base world block

> retro-industrial science fiction, mechanically plausible machinery, massive engineered structures, painted steel, exposed mechanisms, physical controls, maintenance markings, worn surfaces

### Rendering block

> hand-painted late-20th-century Japanese OVA aesthetic, hard cel shading, graphic shadow shapes, selective ink outlines, painted highlights, subtle analog grain

### Environment block

Variable.

Example:

> tropical hydroelectric city built into a jungle canyon, enormous turbines and spillways, vines growing over industrial infrastructure

### Composition block

Variable.

Example:

> wide establishing shot, tiny human figures emphasizing scale, humid atmospheric perspective

### Mood block

Variable.

Example:

> quiet early morning after rainfall

That produces consistency without cloning previous images.

---

# 31. Style-strength slider

For generation, it may be useful to think in terms of a conceptual style intensity.

### Level 1 — subtle

World design follows the grammar, rendering mostly realistic.

### Level 2 — balanced

Strong graphic shadows and stylization, believable materials.

### Level 3 — pronounced

Clearly resembles vintage painted animation.

### Level 4 — extreme

Poster-like, simplified, intentionally graphic.

This lets cinematics, gameplay, concept art, and UI use slightly different levels without becoming separate visual identities.

---

# 32. The 80/20 novelty rule

For most new concepts:

**80% established grammar  
20% novel element**

For major discoveries:

**40% established grammar  
60% unfamiliar**

The more narratively unusual something is, the more visual rules it may violate.

---

# 33. Quick approval checklist

For any concept, ask:

1. Does the silhouette work without detail?
2. Does it feel physically constructed?
3. Can I infer how it functions?
4. Are the materials believable?
5. Is detail concentrated intelligently?
6. Does it look used rather than randomly dirty?
7. Is there a clear visual hierarchy?
8. Does it avoid generic modern sci-fi?
9. Does it fit the world without looking identical to existing designs?
10. Is there at least one memorable idea?

If 8–10 answers are yes, the concept is probably strong.

---

# 34. What I would develop next

The bible is now broad enough to establish a world, but it's still abstract. The most useful next step would be to **stress-test it visually**.

I'd create perhaps six deliberately different concept pieces:

- massive military city
- rural farming settlement
- jungle industrial complex
- orbital spacecraft interior
- character/mecha encounter
- strange alien/ancient structure

If all six look unmistakably like the same game without feeling repetitive, the grammar is working.

At that point we can refine this into something much closer to a real production art bible: **visual reference sheets, faction grammars, palette sheets, material libraries, environment archetypes, character proportions, UI rules, and reusable GenAI prompt templates.**

# Appendix A. Visual-test decisions — 2026-09-05

The six stress tests proposed in section 34 have now been built and rendered successfully in local ComfyUI: massive military city, rural farming settlement, jungle industrial complex, orbital spacecraft interior, character/mecha encounter, and strange alien/ancient structure. A seventh preset generates an isolated mecha source image for later 3D work.

## Generation baseline

- Primary model: Krea 2 Turbo FP8, using `krea2_turbo_fp8_scaled.safetensors`.
- Text encoder: `qwen3vl_4b_fp8_scaled.safetensors`, CLIPLoader type `krea2`.
- VAE: `qwen_image_vae.safetensors`.
- Sampling: Euler / simple, 8 steps, CFG 1, denoise 1, batch size 1.
- Scene resolution: 1344 × 768. Isolated asset: 1024 × 1024.
- Seed: 9052026 for the six scenes; 9052027 for the revised isolated asset. Fixed seed for controlled comparisons; increment for exploration.
- Prompt blocks: world style, rendering style, environment, composition, mood. Keep the first two consistent across presets. These are copied blocks, not globally linked values.
- No automatic prompt expansion or style LoRA. Negative conditioning is inactive at CFG 1.
- ComfyUI workflow folder: `OVA_Bible`. Generated PNGs include workflow metadata.

Krea 2 Turbo was selected for creative stylistic exploration, fast iteration and existing local availability. Qwen-Image-2512 and FLUX.2-dev were evaluated through official documentation as possible future specialists; they were not benchmarked against Krea locally. No secondary model was needed for this test set. The native nodes and three required model files were already installed.

## Findings and production implications

The scene studies maintained a cohesive painted OVA treatment across different scales and regional palettes. The rural study stayed quiet and pastoral; the military city and ancient structure conveyed mass. Some requested details were omitted or changed, so these remain concept studies rather than final production assets.

The isolated mecha preset was revised after the first render cropped its antenna; the final render includes the complete silhouette and background margin. Independent text-only generations preserve visual language more reliably than exact identity. Use reference-conditioned editing for the same approved character or machine across shots and views.

Scene plates are references for later image-to-video work. For Meshy source images, prefer one isolated complete subject, a plain background, visible joints, neutral pose, restrained perspective and even illumination. A scene plate is not a clean single-object 3D input. No Meshy upload, rigging or H3/Minimax animation was performed; downstream compatibility remains to be tested.
